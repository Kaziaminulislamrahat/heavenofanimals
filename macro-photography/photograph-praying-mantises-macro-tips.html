Early on, I made the mistake of treating mantises as if they’d sit quietly for a portrait. Instead, every move I made, shifting my weight, raising my camera, would get a reaction. Sometimes all I’d see were retreating legs or an empty twig. The turning point was realizing each mantis is actively sizing me up, deciding if I’m safe to ignore or something to avoid.

When to Find Mantises That Will Actually Let You Shoot

Morning Is Your Best Friend

The most cooperative mantises are out in the early morning before sunlight warms them up. They’re sluggish from cool air and dew. If you see condensation on leaves or sleepy bees nearby, it’s the perfect time, they’re less likely to spook, so you have longer to compose your shot.

By midday, especially if it’s warm and breezy, mantises are much more alert. I’ve lost count of how many times I tried sneaking up on one in afternoon heat, only for it to vanish within seconds because every movement set off its alarm bells. Now I save those efforts for captive insects or just use the time to scout locations.

Look Where Insects Gather

Mantises don’t just wander aimlessly; they hang out where prey is plentiful, herb gardens, wildflower patches, or spots thick with flying insects. If you spot one, hang back for a few minutes and watch what it’s doing: is it hunting (with those raptorial forelegs cocked), cleaning itself, or resting? Relaxed behavior usually means more tolerance.

For instance, once in my grandmother’s garden, a mantis ignored bees buzzing overhead because she was focused on ants below her stem perch. That gave me space to move in close at ground level, she barely noticed me.

How to Approach Without Scaring Them Off

Mantises react strongly to sudden movement and shadows. After too many failed attempts from moving too fast (and getting nothing but empty shots), I switched tactics:

  1. Pause at a distance: Stop twice as far away as you plan to shoot from and let the mantis notice you.
  2. Advance slowly: Move forward only six inches at a time.
  3. Check your shadow: Hold out your hand first, you want your shadow to fall away from the insect whenever possible.
  4. Watch their body language: Fast antenna flicks or freezing mean they’re nervous; grooming or slow scanning means they’re starting to accept you.

If they go back to normal routine after you pause, grooming legs or calmly looking around, it’s safe to start adjusting composition.

Camera Settings That Work With Unpredictable Subjects

Mantises often move their heads quickly when tracking you or prey nearby. Autofocus can lag behind these tiny movements; manual focus lets you anticipate where their eyes will land.

  • Aperture: Try f/8–f/11 for enough depth of field that slight head turns stay sharp.
  • Shutter speed: Use at least 1/250s if antennae are twitching; only lower it if they’re completely relaxed.
  • ISO: Don’t get hung up on noise, increase ISO until motion blur disappears in test shots.

Illustrative example: A friend once insisted on shooting wide open at f/2.8 for background blur but lost sharpness every time his subject twitched its head. He finally got crisp eye detail by stopping down and waiting for still moments between movements.

Troubleshooting Missed Focus: Behavior Tells You More Than Gear

Blurry eyes? Before blaming your lens:

  • Did the mantis move after you focused?
  • Was autofocus struggling because there wasn’t enough contrast?
  • Did you expect stillness while the insect was obviously hunting?

I now check antennae first; even tiny flicks can signal a shift is coming any second.

Lighting Without Spooking Your Subject

Every lighting choice changes both your results and how safe the mantis feels:

  • Natural light: Least intrusive, try side or backlight for texture without casting harsh shadows over the insect.
  • Flash (diffused): Only use after testing tolerance with a low-power pop from further away. If there’s no reaction (no flinch, no change in posture), try again closer, but stop if they seem uneasy.
  • Reflectors: My favorite trick is holding a white card below them to bounce soft light under their chin instead of using flash overhead, which often scares them.

If you notice repeated defensive moves (like raised forelegs), switch approaches right away instead of pushing through.

Matching Composition To Their Mood

Go with what the mantis gives you:

  • If it’s grooming calmly or just watching its surroundings: Get down low for direct portraits.
  • If it’s upright and tense: Try side profiles that show off its alertness.
  • If it’s hunting: Pull back wider so both predator and potential prey appear in-frame, for storytelling impact.

I used to force my favorite composition even when the mood had changed mid-session; now I watch for shifts and adjust style instead of missing opportunities altogether.

What Your Misses Teach You

Every blurry photo has a reason behind it:

  • Did impatience make you rush?
  • Did ignoring subtle warning signs cost you a shot?
  • Were you so focused on settings that you missed behavioral cues?

If most shots are blurry despite good technique, suspect micro-movements from approaching too quickly, or perhaps tension built up during your session that made them jumpy.

Respect Means Knowing When To Back Off

If your presence keeps triggering defensive reactions, raised forelegs, quick escapes, take it as genuine feedback rather than personal failure. Sometimes leaving them alone is best for both subject and future photoshoots.

Ready-To-Go Checklist For Your Next Mantis Session

  1. Scout early mornings when insects move slowly.
  2. Look for “insect highways”, floral patches or dense greenery.
  3. Watch behavior before approaching; pause if they seem tense.
  4. Move slowly in small steps; check body language after each advance.
  5. Use manual focus if possible; set aperture around f/8–f/11.
  6. Test lighting gently; prefer natural light or soft reflectors first.
  7. Adapt composition based on current behavior, don’t force poses.
  8. If repeated stress shows up, give them space and try another day.
  9. Review mistakes, was it timing, approach, settings, or reading signals?
  10. Most important: enjoy watching them as much as photographing them!

Even now, I still miss shots sometimes, but every outing teaches me something new about these remarkable little hunters and how best to photograph them up close.

You’ll find that tuning into their world makes every image, and every story behind it, that much more rewarding.
