Mantises: What They Hunt (and What They Don’t Care About)

Mantises are generalist predators. This means they’ll catch any insect they can grab, caterpillars, grasshoppers, moths, aphids, but also butterflies, bees, and even each other. They don’t target just pests. If it moves and fits in their jaws, it’s fair game.

When does this help?

  • If your main problem is with slow or soft-bodied pests (like cabbage worms or leafhoppers), mantises might reduce those populations.
  • If your garden is full of pollinators or helpful bugs (bees, ladybugs), expect some losses there too.

Bottom line: Mantises work best where pest insects dominate and beneficial insects are less common or can tolerate some losses.

Attracting Wild Mantises vs. Buying Egg Cases

Attracting Wild Mantises

Mantises will only settle where the environment feels right: lots of plants at different heights, steady food supply, places to lay eggs safely. In wild areas or older neighborhoods with mixed plantings, you might see them move in naturally over time.

  • What helps: Shrubs and perennials mixed with annuals; patches of unmowed or undisturbed ground; leaving dead stems through winter.
  • What won’t work: Bare beds, all turf lawns, or gardens cleaned up too thoroughly every fall.

This approach is slow. It can take years to build up a self-sustaining population if you start from scratch.

Introducing Egg Cases (“Oothecae”)

If you buy mantis egg cases and place them out in spring, you get a burst of nymphs hatching at once. But most will not survive: many are eaten by birds or each other within days if there’s not enough cover or food.

Composite example:
One spring I tied two egg cases to exposed tomato stakes in a bare patch. Hatch day was thrilling, hundreds of tiny mantids spilled out. By the end of the week? Not a single one left. The beds were too open; local sparrows had an easy feast and there was nowhere for nymphs to hide.

Lesson: Without thick plant cover at ground level (think clumps of low perennials or uncut stems), most hatchlings vanish before they get big enough to hunt pests.

How Garden Structure Changes Your Results

Young mantises need dense foliage for both hunting and hiding from predators like birds. Gardens made up mostly of wide open spaces or neatly spaced rows (with bare soil between) will almost always fail to support them past the nymph stage.

What actually works:

  • Plant clusters of shrubs or tall perennials every few feet instead of just straight rows.
  • Leave some leaf litter under plants, this gives hatchlings shelter.
  • Hold off on heavy cleanup until after winter so egg cases aren’t accidentally tossed out with debris.

If you rarely see adult mantids by midsummer:
Your garden probably lacks enough cover, not necessarily a problem with the egg cases themselves.

When to Place Egg Cases Outside

Timing matters more than most guides suggest. Praying mantis eggs only hatch when nighttime temperatures stay above 50°F (10°C) for at least a week straight. Cold snaps before this point can kill embryos inside the case.

Checklist before setting out egg cases:

  • Monitor overnight lows (use a basic thermometer if needed).
  • Wait until at least 7 nights have stayed above 50°F.
  • Attach cases 1–2 feet off the ground on sturdy plant stems or shrub branches near dense foliage.
  • Avoid areas that flood or get direct irrigation spray, wet eggs rot easily.

If a late cold snap hits after placing eggs:
Move any remaining oothecae to a sheltered spot like a south-facing wall or inside dense shrubs if possible.

How to Tell If Mantises Are Making a Difference

Don’t expect crowds of adult mantids, they’re solitary by nature, and survival rates are low even under ideal conditions (5–10% is typical).

Instead, look for:

  • Fewer visible clusters of key pests over several weeks
  • Less obvious chewing damage on leaves
  • Occasional sightings of juvenile mantids moving among plants during peak pest season

If your pollinator numbers drop sharply (fewer bees/butterflies), mantises might be taking too many non-pest insects. In that case, reconsider whether this approach fits your goals.

Diagnosing Common Problems

Here’s how to troubleshoot if things don’t go as planned:

  1. No nymphs seen after expected hatch time:
    Usually bird predation or lack of ground-level cover is to blame.
  2. Pests still everywhere after release:
    Small gardens may be too limited for mantis numbers to help much. Try hand-picking pests while improving habitat structure.
  3. Nymphs eat each other right away:
    Normal at high density; spread egg cases farther apart next year so hatchlings disperse.
  4. Sudden disappearance mid-season:
    Even “organic” sprays can kill young mantids through residue contact, avoid spraying while nymphs are present unless absolutely necessary.

When Praying Mantises Are Not the Right Tool

If protecting pollinators is your top concern, for example in butterfly gardens, mantises may do more harm than good due to their indiscriminate appetite. For urgent pest outbreaks threatening crops before natural controls catch up, physical barriers like row covers plus targeted hand-removal work faster and more reliably than releasing generalist predators alone.

Your Next Step: A Quick Garden Audit

Before buying egg cases:

  1. Walk your garden bed by bed.
  2. Score each area for three things:
  • How many types of plants? (Diversity)
  • Is there thick perennial cover at multiple heights?
  • Any recent pesticide use?
  1. Fix weak spots first: add dense perennials/shrubs where bare; stop using sprays; leave some leaf litter until spring warms up fully.

Improving structure does far more for future mantis survival than simply adding more eggs every year, and it benefits all kinds of helpful insects as well.

Growing healthy populations of praying mantises is about patience and habitat, not quick fixes from a box at the store. With the right adjustments, you’ll give these fascinating hunters a real shot at helping keep your pest problems in check, without losing sight of what else lives in your garden too.
