Giraffes are famous for eating leaves from tall trees, acacia is the classic example. In the wild, they’ll reach up to six meters high, using their long, prehensile tongues to strip off foliage that most other herbivores can’t access. But their diets are more varied and complicated than a simple list suggests, especially in managed care.

Here’s a direct answer:
Wild giraffes eat mostly leaves from acacia, mimosa, and combretum trees. They also browse on twigs, buds, flowers, seedpods, and sometimes fruit. In zoos and sanctuaries, the diet often includes fresh browse from safe local trees (like willow or mulberry), hay for fiber, and specially-formulated pellets for nutrients.

Let’s break down what this looks like day-to-day, and what matters most if you’re responsible for feeding giraffes.

Why Giraffes Need More Than Just Greenery

Giraffes aren’t general grazers. Their bodies are built to browse, meaning they eat leaves and soft shoots from woody plants, not grass. Their tongues (about 45 centimeters long) are tough enough to work around thorns and pick out the tender parts of branches.

This isn’t just about taste:

  • Browsing reduces competition: Most herbivores eat lower or on the ground. Giraffes’ height lets them reach food others leave untouched.
  • Digestive needs: Their rumen (the first stomach chamber) functions best with fibrous browse that keeps digestion moving.

If a giraffe ignores fresh hay in favor of branches, or seems uninterested in provided browse, this often signals a mismatch between what’s offered and what they instinctively need.

Group Feeding: How Social Order Impacts Who Eats What

In any group of giraffes, feeding time is rarely fair. Dominant adults get first pick at the best branches; younger or less assertive animals often end up with tougher or less nutritious leftovers.

Composite example: At several zoos I’ve visited during winter shortages, keepers distributed willow branches among five giraffes. The two oldest females immediately claimed the highest piles while juveniles lingered on the edges until only thin twigs remained. Over months like this, staff noticed uneven tooth wear and some slow growth in subordinates.

What works:

  • Move branch placements daily so all animals get a shot at prime spots.
  • Track who spends time on which foods; use this to adjust future feedings.
  • Watch for signs like overgrown teeth or stunted growth, these often trace back to social exclusion at meals.

The Hay-and-Pellet Shortcut: Hidden Risks

It’s tempting (especially outside Africa) to rely on hay and commercial herbivore pellets as dietary staples because they’re easy to store and measure. But this approach can backfire:

  • Digestive slowdown: Without coarse browse to chew on, rumen activity falls off, leading to bloat or even acidosis.
  • Dental problems: Hay alone won’t wear teeth down properly; over time this means more dental care.
  • Boredom behaviors: When natural foraging is limited, captive giraffes develop habits like tongue rolling or pacing out of frustration.

One facility I know (composite example) switched almost entirely to alfalfa hay during a harsh winter. Vet bills rose, mostly dental issues, and staff saw more anxious behavior until they could bring back local cut branches.

Feeding Giraffes Outside Africa: Practical Workarounds

It’s nearly impossible to provide African acacia year-round anywhere else due to climate and import rules. Here’s how experienced staff adapt:

  • Local substitutes: Willow and mulberry branches are widely used stand-ins for African species; they aren’t identical but offer similar roughage.
  • Stockpiling: Some facilities freeze or dry extra browse gathered in summer so it can be offered through winter when fresh options run low.
  • Pellet adjustment: Monitor actual intake of fresh browse before adjusting pellet rations, overdoing pellets leads quickly to obesity.

Aim for at least three safe tree types rotated weekly rather than relying on one favorite species all season.

“People Food” Isn’t Safe by Default

Fruits and vegetables might seem harmless as treats, but giraffe metabolism doesn’t handle sugar well:

  • Carrots can cause digestive upset if fed in large amounts.
  • Bananas have even higher sugar; repeated use quickly leads to hoof issues or metabolic spikes.

If you notice lameness or gut changes after introducing produce treats, pull back immediately. Use fruits only as rare rewards during training, not as regular snacks.

Build a Realistic Plan With What You Have

No one can replicate wild African diets exactly, adaptability matters most:

  1. Watch carefully who eats what every day; adjust branch placement as needed so no one gets left out.
  2. Offer as much variety in safe local browse as possible alongside hay.
  3. Limit treats strictly to enrichment activities or medical training, and always check for reactions before repeating.
  4. Review your feeding logs each year against credible guidelines like those from zoo nutrition groups.

Highest Impact: Expand Your Browse Sources First

Before tweaking supplements or shopping for new pellets:

  • List every branch type you’ve offered over the last month.
  • Look for gaps when only hay was available.
  • Identify one new safe local tree (or shrub) you could add next season, even small increases in diversity go a long way toward better health.

When you focus on practical decisions, like broadening browse options and tracking social dynamics, you’ll see healthier animals with fewer behavior issues and vet visits. Every branch you offer is a chance to support your giraffes’ wellbeing, and with regular observation and small adjustments, you’ll build expertise that guides better choices over time.

If you need specific lists of safe trees for your area or want advice on monitoring group feeding habits, reach out, you don’t have to solve every problem alone.
