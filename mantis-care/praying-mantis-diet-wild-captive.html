Quick Reference: What Mantises Eat

Wild Diet:

  • Small flying insects: flies (house flies, bluebottles), moths, bees
  • Crawling insects: crickets, grasshoppers, caterpillars
  • Occasional spiders or even other mantises (especially young nymphs)

Captive Diet:

  • House flies (best for most species and sizes)
  • Fruit flies (essential for hatchlings and tiny nymphs)
  • Pinhead crickets or small locusts
  • Waxworms, small roaches (Dubia), silkworms
    Avoid mealworms except as an occasional treat; they can bite back.

Why Feeding Mantises Isn’t “Just Any Bug”

It seems simple, offer a bug, watch your mantis hunt. But real issues begin when prey size or type is off. I still remember offering a too-large cricket to my first subadult mantis. She ignored it for days and later failed to molt properly. That lesson stuck.

Common Mistakes:

  • Prey too big: Intimidates or injures the mantis.
  • Wrong movement style: Many prefer flying prey over crawling bugs.
  • Low activity: Mantises rarely take dead or sluggish insects.

What works best:
Live house flies or fruit flies for most species. Only offer what your mantis can overpower easily, a good rule is prey no longer than half your mantis's body.

When Your Mantis Stops Eating: What to Check First

If your mantis ignores food for more than a day outside of molting cycles, something’s off. Here’s a quick checklist:

  1. Is the prey alive and active?
    Sluggish bugs are usually ignored.
  2. Is the prey an appropriate size?
    Too big = intimidation; too small = disinterest.
  3. Are you offering the right kind?
    Nymphs often ignore crickets; try flies instead.

Illustrative example:
A new keeper gives mealworms because they’re cheap and available. The mantis won't eat them, and after a few tries, one mealworm bites back, stressing the mantis further. Switching to small house flies usually fixes appetite within a day.

Feeding Schedule: How Much Is Too Much?

Unlike mammals, mantises don’t need daily meals.

Typical schedule:

  • Nymphs (young): feed every 1–2 days
  • Adults: every 2–3 days

Signs of overfeeding: bloated abdomen, regurgitation, eventually refusing food entirely. Underfeeding shows up fast, thin abdomen and trouble molting.

Tip: Always check the shape of your mantis’s belly:

  • Slightly rounded is perfect.
  • Sunken = hungry.
  • Swollen = cut back on feedings.

Why Wild-Caught Insects Aren’t Always Safe

It’s tempting to scoop up bugs from outside (I’ve done this myself). But wild insects may carry pesticides or parasites, both can be fatal.

Red flags after new feeder source:

  • Lethargy within two days
  • Black spots on body
  • Sudden swelling

If you notice any of these after switching food types collected outdoors, stop immediately and switch to commercially raised feeders. They’re far safer overall.

When Appetite Vanishes: Environmental Triggers

Sometimes your setup, not just the diet, is sabotaging hunger:

  • Temperature below 22°C/72°F: Mantises become sluggish and ignore food.
  • Humidity below 60 percent: Makes hunting difficult; also impairs molting.

Always check enclosure temperature and humidity if appetite drops unexpectedly, especially after weather changes indoors.

Molting vs. Illness: Don’t Force Feed!

A classic mistake is panicking during pre-molt fasting. If your mantis has stopped eating but its abdomen looks plump and color dulls slightly, it’s preparing to molt! Leave it alone until it sheds successfully.

If it still won’t eat three days after molting, or with no sign of molt at all, review all of the above steps again, focusing first on prey type and enclosure conditions.

Easiest Fix: Offer One Live Commercial Insect and Watch Closely

Not sure what’s wrong? My go-to test:

  1. Place one small live house fly or pinhead cricket in the enclosure (from a commercial supplier).
  2. Watch for hunting behavior within an hour.
  3. If ignored, check temperature/humidity and note last molt date.

This single step quickly tells you if diet or environment needs adjusting, and prevents health decline before it gets serious.

Few things are more satisfying than seeing a praying mantis stalk its prey with full confidence! Most feeding issues are fixable with careful observation and some trial-and-error around food type and environment. Every keeper runs into these challenges, the key is staying curious and tweaking until you see that perfect hunting response again.

If you’re ever unsure about what to offer next, return to basics: live house flies for adults, fruit flies for babies, correct temperature and humidity, and don’t stress over missed meals right before a molt. With patience (and maybe a little stubbornness, I know I needed both), even tricky feeders usually come around!
