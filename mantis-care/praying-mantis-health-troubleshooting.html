Most mantis health issues aren’t mysterious diseases, they’re environmental hiccups that respond well to practical troubleshooting.

Below, I’ll walk you through the most common mantis care problems, how to spot them, and what steps actually work. These are based on patterns I’ve seen repeat themselves in real-world keeping, including a few missteps of my own.

Why Most Mantis “Illness” Is a Simple Fix

If your mantis is acting strange, slow, not eating, or looking shriveled, the solution is usually environmental. True infections are rare in captivity. Racing for exotic cures wastes time and can make things worse.

Start with these basics every time:

  • Temperature: If it drops below 22°C (72°F), mantises slow down. Use a thermometer right inside the enclosure.
  • Humidity: Below 60% causes dehydration and molting trouble. A digital hygrometer helps here.
  • Cleanliness: Leftover food or waste quickly breeds mites and mold.
  • Prey Size: Prey should be about a third as long as your mantis’s body.

This approach rules out 90% of problems in minutes.

Hygiene: The Overlooked Time Bomb

Even careful keepers can miss a hidden cricket or patch of mold. In one composite example: a keeper didn’t notice an uneaten roach buried under moss for days. Soon after, tiny mites appeared and her mantis became listless. Only a full cleaning and substrate change cleared it up.

Routine that works:

  • Remove uneaten prey within 12 hours.
  • Spot-clean for waste daily.
  • Swap substrate every week or two; rinse decor with hot water (no soap).
  • If you see mites (tiny moving dots) or mold (white fuzz), clean immediately and replace affected materials.

Molting Problems: Almost Always Preventable

Over 80% of failed molts happen because humidity is too low or there isn’t enough space to climb and hang.

Checklist for smooth molts:

  • Keep humidity between 60–80%, depending on species.
  • Give at least three times the mantis’s body length in vertical climbing space with rough branches or mesh.
  • Don’t move or handle your mantis when it looks swollen or sluggish before a molt.

If a molt goes wrong, like skin stuck on the abdomen, fix humidity and climbing options before the next shed. Young nymphs sometimes regrow lost limbs in future molts; adults cannot.

Illustrative example: I once had a nymph lose two legs after getting stuck during a dry molt. After boosting humidity and adding more branches, its next molt was perfect, and it even regrew one leg.

Appetite Drop? Usually Nothing Serious

A mantis refusing food is rarely an emergency unless other symptoms appear.

Ask yourself:

  • Is it about to molt? Fasting before shedding is normal.
  • Is the prey too big or too small? Try live prey about one-third their size.
  • Has the temperature dropped? Even slight chills slow feeding.
  • Is there noise or bright light at night? Reduce disturbances if possible.

If your juvenile hasn’t eaten for five days (or an adult for ten), check for dehydration, stuck molts, or odd color changes, and review all enclosure conditions.

Real Symptoms With Real Solutions

Lethargic With Dark Spots

A composite scenario: A mantis kept near a cold window started acting sluggish with brownish joints. Raising the temperature from 16°C (61°F) up to 25°C (77°F) brought it back to normal within two days.

Stuck Molt

One young mantis repeatedly had trouble shedding its skin until its keeper raised humidity from 40% to above 65% and added textured branches. After that, every molt succeeded.

Sudden Death After Leaving Prey Overnight

An adult died after sharing its enclosure with an uneaten cricket overnight, crickets can bite resting mantises. Always remove uneaten prey promptly, especially anything aggressive like crickets or large roaches. I learned this lesson myself after losing a favorite female to an unnoticed cricket bite early on in my keeping days.

When Basics Aren’t Enough: Next Steps

Most problems improve fast once you fix temperature, humidity, cleanliness, and climbing space. If your mantis still has symptoms after correcting these basics:

  1. Take clear photos showing symptoms and setup details.
  2. Note dates and any recent changes.
  3. Share this info with experienced keepers online for targeted advice.

You’re not alone, almost every keeper has faced these challenges at some point, myself included. With steady attention to the fundamentals, you’ll get past most bumps quickly and your mantises will thrive.
