Expectant human parents frequently talk, read, or sing lullabies to their bumps to build an early connection. As it turns out, bottlenose dolphins share a remarkably similar maternal instinct: dolphin mothers sing their personal "names" to their calves while still in the womb.

What Is a Signature Whistle?

Every bottlenose dolphin possesses a distinct vocalization known as a signature whistle. Because underwater visibility is often limited and pods move quickly across open waters, these acoustic markers function much like names. They allow individuals to broadcast their identity, coordinate group movement, and locate one another across vast distances.

While adult dolphins maintain an individualized whistle for life, newborn calves are not born with their own fully formed identity call. Instead, their earliest vocal lessons come directly from mom.

The Science Behind the Song

Researchers investigating cetacean acoustic behavior tracked vocalizations within a dolphin pod around the arrival of a newborn calf. By analyzing 80 hours of acoustic recordings taken across the two months before and two months following birth, scientists uncovered a deliberate shifts in communication:

  • The Two-Week Pre-Birth Surge: Roughly two weeks before giving birth, the mother dolphin drastically increased the repetition of her signature whistle.
  • The Post-Birth Reinforcement: She sustained this heightened frequency through the calf’s first two weeks of life before gradually tapering off.
  • The Imprinting Window: Just as human babies develop a neurological preference for their mother's voice during the third trimester, marine biologists hypothesize that this vocal surge acts as an imprinting mechanism. It ensures the newborn calf immediately recognizes its mother amid the chaotic acoustics of the ocean.

A Quiet Village: Pod Solidarity

Perhaps the most fascinating finding from the research was the reaction of the surrounding pod.

While the expectant mother amplified her calls, the other dolphins in the enclosure noticeably lowered their own whistle rates. Biologists believe this quiet period was deliberate: by keeping vocal "noise" to a minimum, the pod prevented the vulnerable calf from imprinting on the wrong signature whistle. Only after the calf had solidified its bond with the mother did the rest of the pod resume their standard vocal frequencies.

The Power of Cetacean Connection

In the animal kingdom, few bonds rival the maternal investment of cetaceans. Teaching a calf to recognize a single, vital acoustic frequency before it even enters the world highlights just how emotionally and socially sophisticated these marine mammals truly are.
