Can Meerkats Be Pets? Legal, Ethical, and Practical Realities

The Reality Behind the Appeal

Most people who think about getting a meerkat aren’t looking for trouble, they just want a deeper connection with an animal they admire. I get that. But there’s a huge gap between enjoying an animal from afar and meeting its real needs up close. The biggest mistake is assuming you can change an animal’s nature by caring “enough.” One friend (in a composite example) tried this with another exotic species. Within weeks: destroyed furniture, unpredictable aggression, and an anxious animal that never really settled in.

This happens over and over because our good intentions can’t erase behaviors shaped by evolution or fix environments built for humans instead of wildlife.

How Meerkats Behave When Kept Alone

Meerkats are not loners. In the wild, every part of their day revolves around their group, the “mob.” They rely on each other for safety, grooming, play, and learning how to handle threats. When one is kept alone:

  • It often becomes withdrawn or fixates on repetitive pacing.
  • Attempts at cuddling or solo play usually increase stress rather than comfort.
  • Digging and scent-marking ramp up quickly when social interaction is missing.

This isn’t bad luck; it’s simply unmet needs expressing themselves in obvious ways. Unless you can provide a full meerkat group and meet their need for constant social complexity (which almost no private person can), the animal will suffer, and you’ll end up frustrated.

Why Laws Are So Strict, and What They Actually Mean for You

You might hear someone say they’ve seen meerkats as pets on social media or even know someone who claims to have one. In most places, that’s only possible by breaking the law or jumping through serious hoops.

If you’re considering this idea, start here:

  1. Find your state or country wildlife agency website (or call your local animal control office). Search specifically for “prohibited species” or “exotic pet permits.”
  2. Ask: Is private ownership of meerkats allowed? In most regions: no license means no legal way to keep one.
  3. If permits exist: Check what standards you’d have to meet, these usually include inspections, secure enclosures, proof of experience, and ongoing oversight.
  4. Ask local veterinarians if they treat meerkats (not just exotics generally). If none do within driving distance, treat that as a dead end.

These laws aren’t just red tape, they’re built on decades of problems with escaped animals, disease risk, neglected individuals needing rescue, and public safety issues.

The Real Ethical Stakes: Animal Welfare Comes First

Even if you could get around legal barriers (which you shouldn’t), the ethical questions remain:

  • Keeping a single meerkat almost always means chronic loneliness.
  • Captive-bred doesn’t equal domesticated; stress behaviors like self-harm and depression still appear.
  • Every purchase creates demand for more breeding or illegal wild capture.

The bottom line: If your plan relies on being “the exception,” take a step back. Ask yourself if any part of your approach truly matches what’s proven to work for these animals, not just what feels hopeful.

Why Training Doesn’t Fix Fundamental Problems

Dogs and cats fit into our homes because thousands of years ago we started selecting them for traits that made them adaptable companions: less fear of humans, ease with new environments, manageable instincts. Meerkats missed out on all of that selective breeding.

What happens instead?

  • Digging cannot be trained away; it’s instinctive.
  • Scent marking will happen everywhere, it isn’t “bad behavior,” it’s normal.
  • Biting may emerge when they’re scared or confused; it isn’t personal but can be serious.

Even professionals working in zoos focus on enrichment and management, not turning meerkats into pets.

What To Do Instead: Safe Ways To Connect With Meerkats

If your interest is about learning or connecting with these animals (and not about owning something unusual), there are options that actually help both you and them:

  • Visit reputable zoos or sanctuaries where groups live together as they should.
  • Support conservation programs; many offer symbolic adoptions or special behind-the-scenes experiences.
  • Share what you’ve learned with friends who might be tempted by exotic pets, real knowledge changes minds better than warnings alone.

A Simple Test Before Going Any Further

Here’s one practical filter that stops most bad pet decisions before they start:

Try to find a veterinarian within an hour’s drive willing and able to provide long-term care for a meerkat. If you can’t find one (and chances are high you won’t), let that reality guide your decision.

Taking care of these animals isn’t impossible, but it requires resources far beyond what most individuals can access safely or ethically.

Most people who ask about keeping meerkats as pets really want connection or meaning in their relationship with animals, and there are ways to get those things without putting yourself (or a wild creature) in an impossible spot. If you stay curious but respect the limits set by biology and law, both you and the animals come out ahead.
