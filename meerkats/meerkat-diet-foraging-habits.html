The first time I watched a meerkat group spread out across the Kalahari sand, the simplicity of their mission was obvious. Noses down, claws flying, each animal was after the same prize: something wriggling just below the surface. Most books will tell you meerkats are “insectivores that also eat some plants and small animals,” but if you spend a few hours with them, you realize how much that glosses over. Here’s what truly matters about what they eat and how they find it, lessons learned through sandy boots and quiet observation.

Insects Are Everything

A meerkat’s day revolves around insects. Almost every successful foraging moment I’ve seen ends with a grub or beetle larva snapped up and swallowed in seconds. Termite mounds are prime real estate after rain. Sometimes I’d spot a young meerkat digging for minutes with single-minded focus; when she finally pulled out a fat larva, her persistence made sense. The payoff is high protein and low risk.

Other foods, like lizards, scorpions, or bird eggs, do show up in their diet, but only occasionally. These items come with more danger (think: venomous prey or angry parent birds) and take more effort to catch. Meerkats don’t go looking for variety; they stick to what works.

If you care for meerkats or teach about them: Make insect prey your top priority. Live insects like crickets, mealworms, or roaches aren’t just treats, they’re essential for health and natural behavior. Fruits and meats belong as rare exceptions.

Foraging Is a Group Effort, But Not Without Tension

A group of meerkats on the hunt moves almost in sync, each animal focused on its patch of sand but never straying too far from the others. There’s a rhythm: dig, sniff, paw the ground again. Every few minutes, someone pops up to scan for hawks, a job that rotates among adults.

I’ve noticed that in larger groups (ten or more), everyone seems less anxious; sentinels switch off smoothly so others can dig longer without looking up as often. In smaller mobs, or after a predator scare, the mood changes fast. Meerkats become jumpy, abandon good digging spots, or bicker over scraps because no one wants to be caught off guard.

If you see repeated interruptions at feeding time in captivity: Check if group sizes are small or if there’s been recent stress (loud noises, new animals nearby). Often it’s anxiety about safety, not lack of food, that’s causing the disruption.

What Happens When We Get Their Diets Wrong

Here’s an illustrative example from a sanctuary visit: Staff had been told that “more variety makes for happier animals.” They offered fruit slices and even cooked chicken alongside bugs. Within months, several meerkats developed dental problems and started gaining unhealthy weight.

In nature, meerkats almost never eat sugary food. Their teeth are built for crunching shells, not soft treats. Too much fruit leads straight to tooth decay and metabolic issues.

Best correction: Feed mostly live insects. Avoid fruits or processed foods except as very rare exceptions, and only in tiny amounts if used at all.

Scarcity Is Normal, And Shapes How They Eat

Some days in meerkat country are tough even for expert diggers. After weeks without rain or in areas grazed bare by livestock, insects become scarce. I’ve watched entire groups pick listlessly at roots or scratch at hard earth before giving up by midday.

They aren’t choosing these fallback foods; they’re making do until conditions improve. In captivity this means enrichment isn’t just about food, it’s also about letting them dig and work for meals.

If your captive meerkats seem bored or obsessed with anything novel: Try giving them more chances to dig (sand trays work well) along with frequent insect delivery before adding new foods to their diet plan.

Quick Reference Checklist

For anyone caring for or teaching about meerkats:

  • Feed mainly live insects: Crickets, mealworms, roaches.
  • Limit fruits/sweets to almost never: Only as rare training aids.
  • Encourage digging: Provide sand or soil to mimic wild hunting.
  • Watch group dynamics: Tension often points to stress or safety worries.
  • Observe feeding times closely: Early morning is best for natural behaviors.
  • Log what gets eaten most often: This tells you more than any textbook list.

Meerkat diets aren’t complicated once you see them in action: they thrive as specialists on hidden protein found underfoot, and everything else is just survival improvisation when times are lean. If your approach starts there, with insects first and digging always, you’ll support both healthy bodies and wild instincts.

If you get the chance to watch them yourself (even via webcam), try jotting down what food types appear most often and which behaviors lead to success or frustration. Those patterns reveal what truly matters, for meerkats and those who care for them alike.
