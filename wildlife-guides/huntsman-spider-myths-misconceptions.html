Why Myths About Huntsman Spiders Take Hold

Most people’s fear of huntsman spiders doesn’t come from being bitten or harmed, it starts with a jump scare. When a big, fast spider darts across your wall or ceiling, the instinct is to brace for danger. But that gut reaction often leads to wild stories and stretched truths, especially when mixed with social media headlines and secondhand tales.

In my own neighborhood group, I see far more dramatic “almost attacked!” posts than actual bite reports. Usually, the person hasn’t even touched the spider, and sometimes they’ve misidentified it altogether. The bigger and quicker the spider, the scarier it feels, and the more those stories spread.

Quick Traits: Is That Actually a Huntsman?

Before you panic, check these:

  • Legs stick out to the sides (like a crab), not straight ahead.
  • Flat body shape, can squeeze into cracks.
  • Moves sideways or quickly across walls; rarely seen hanging from silk threads.

If you spot a large spider but it doesn’t match this look, odds are high it’s another species.

Myth: “Huntsman Spiders Are Deadly”

There’s no evidence that huntsman bites are dangerous to humans. Hospitals in Australia (where they’re common) rarely see confirmed huntsman bites at all. When they do, symptoms usually mean mild swelling or redness, nothing life-threatening. Severe allergic reactions can happen with any insect or spider bite, but there’s never been a proven death from a huntsman.

Why their venom isn’t built to hurt people: Huntsmans evolved to catch insects, not defend against mammals like us. Their venom is strong enough for bugs but too weak for serious harm in humans.

Common mistake: People assume that any large spider must be dangerous, leading to unnecessary fear, and sometimes killing spiders that pose no threat.

Are Huntsmans Chasing You? What’s Really Happening

Many people say huntsmans “chased” them around a room. In reality, these spiders run toward darkness and cover when startled, not toward you on purpose.

Composite example:
During a spring cleaning session at home last year, I grabbed a broom to nudge an enormous huntsman off the wall. As soon as I moved in, it sprinted right at me, until I realized I was standing between the spider and its hiding spot under the washing machine. It wasn’t attacking; it just wanted out of sight.

Practical tip:
If you feel pursued, check where you’re standing. Nine times out of ten, you’re blocking its escape route.

Should You Remove or Tolerate Huntsmans Inside?

Many suggest catching huntsmans with a cup and sheet of paper, then releasing them outside. This works in most cases, but isn’t always necessary or best for everyone.

  • If your home struggles with cockroaches or flies: Leaving quiet huntsmans alone in low-traffic areas (garage, laundry) can help control pests without chemicals.
  • If someone in your home is terrified or small children are likely to grab spiders: Relocate them gently.
  • If repeated relocations stress out both you and the spider (or end up injuring it): Pick your battles, consider only removing those in bedrooms or busy spaces.

Try setting up “spider-safe zones.” For example: leave them undisturbed in laundry rooms or garages, but relocate ones found in bedrooms, kitchens, or playrooms. This reduces surprises and helps everyone adjust gradually.

Mistaken Identity: Most Big Spiders Aren’t Dangerous

A lot of indoor spiders get mistaken for something harmful. In most cases:

  • Brown Recluse? Rare outside certain US regions.
  • Funnel-web? Limited range; very different habits.
  • Huntsman? Noticeably flat body and crab-like stance.

Before spraying pesticides or reaching for shoes, double-check using local guides, or snap a photo for your local nature group. Most large spiders indoors are harmless pest hunters.

Do Huntsmans Make Messy Webs? No

Huntsmans don’t build tangled webs around your house. They hunt actively by running down prey rather than trapping it.

If you notice thick cobwebs in corners or window frames, that’s likely another species (such as cellar spiders). A lurking huntsman prefers open surfaces like walls and ceilings, and won’t leave sticky messes behind.

What To Do If You Get Bitten

Bites from huntsmans are rare and almost always mild. Usually they happen when someone grabs one by accident during cleaning, not from an attack.

Care steps:

  1. Wash the area with soap and water.
  2. Use ice if there’s swelling.
  3. Watch for unusual signs like difficulty breathing, severe rash spreading away from the bite site, dizziness, or intense pain.
  4. If any of those happen, or if you’re worried, call your doctor just to be safe.

Most bites just need basic first aid and calm observation at home.

Living With Huntsmans: Pros And Cons

Allowing some huntsmans to stay can cut down on roaches and flies without extra sprays or traps, a win if you hate chemical pest control. But if anyone at home has severe spider fears or allergies, or if pets might tangle with spiders, relocation may be safer for peace of mind.

Think about where discomfort becomes distress: tolerate quietly patrolling spiders in little-used rooms; move them if they turn up somewhere nerve-wracking or unsafe.

How To Handle Future Encounters Calmly

Next time you spot a big spider on your wall:

  1. Take a breath before reacting.
  2. Check its features, is it actually a huntsman?
  3. Decide if it needs moving based on room use and comfort.
  4. If relocating: Use a container-and-paper method instead of harsh sprays.
  5. Pass along what you’ve learned, clear facts help everyone stay calmer next time too.

Every encounter is easier when you know what to expect, and remember that most huntsmans want nothing more than to stay out of your way while quietly taking care of pests you’d rather not see either.

Illustrative Example:
One evening last winter I stumbled into my dark laundry room, and froze as I spotted long legs sprawled near my pile of towels. My heart jumped first; logic came second as I remembered these stories: It moved faster than seemed possible…and vanished into a gap behind the dryer without even glancing my way! That moment finally convinced me, the terror was all mine; the spider just wanted peace and quiet.

With understanding comes calm (most of the time). Knowing what’s myth versus fact lets you choose whether to relocate gently, or simply nod hello and let both of you go about your business safely inside your own home.
