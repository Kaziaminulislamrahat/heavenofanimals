The first time I watched a huntsman spider explore its enclosure, I realized the typical spider care checklist wasn’t enough. Unlike tarantulas that dig and hide, huntsmans climb, sprint, and constantly test their environment. The biggest challenge isn’t their speed or reputation, it’s learning to pay attention to their choices instead of relying on generic advice.

Early on, I tried using a basic terrestrial setup with a burrow hide. Within days, my huntsman ignored the hide and spent every night scaling the corners, looking for vertical surfaces. That was my first lesson: let the spider show you what it needs.

Setting Up an Enclosure Based on Actual Huntsman Behavior

Observation comes first: Watch where your spider spends its evenings. Does it cling to glass, dart between upright branches, or squeeze behind bark? Their behavior tells you exactly how to design their space.

Key mistakes I made at first:

  • Underestimating escape risk.
  • Not providing enough height or climbing options.
  • Overlooking small gaps in the lid.

What works best:

  • Use a tall enclosure (at least 12x12x18 inches). If your huntsman spends most nights perched above ground level, you’ve chosen well.
  • Install smooth but secure vertical elements like cork bark or thick branches.
  • Double-check every seam and latch after feeding or cleaning. An illustrative example: I once had a juvenile slip out through a 2 mm gap near the hinge, now I run my finger along all edges before stepping away.
  • Monitor ventilation by checking for condensation and airflow. If you see your spider holding its legs away from surfaces or only using one corner, airflow might be off.

Feeding: Timing and Quantity Driven by Your Spider’s Actions

Standard advice says feed every few days, but that misses key details. Huntsmans often refuse food right before molting and become much more active right after.

How to decide when and how much:

  • Offer prey at dusk when you notice your spider moving around.
  • Choose feeder insects smaller than the spider’s body length so they don’t intimidate or injure your pet.
  • Remove uneaten prey within 24 hours. Crickets hiding during molts can cause injury, a mistake I made once with an unseen cricket hiding under bark during a molt.

Composite example: One keeper found that her huntsman would only eat if left alone in low light; too much movement outside the enclosure made it ignore food completely. Simple observation revealed this crucial preference.

Adjusting Temperature and Humidity by Watching Activity Levels

Huntsmans do fine at room temperature in summer but slow down in cooler months. When my home got colder in winter, my normally active huntsman became sluggish and refused meals until I raised temperatures slightly with a small heat mat on one side of the enclosure (never underneath).

How to troubleshoot:

  • If your spider is inactive or not eating outside of molt season, measure temperatures both day and night inside the enclosure.
  • During molts, raise humidity modestly only if you see sluggishness or incomplete molts. Don’t soak the whole tank; instead, lightly mist one corner and let the spider choose where it feels comfortable.
  • Over-misting can quickly cause mold (I learned this after having to replace an entire substrate).

Handling: When It’s Worthwhile, and When It Isn’t

Huntsmans are fast movers, handling them for fun almost always leads to stress for both keeper and spider. My own attempt at gentle handling ended with a wild dash onto my shoulder and two minutes of careful coaxing with a catch cup.

Best practice:

  • Skip handling unless absolutely necessary (cleaning or rehoming).
  • If you must move your huntsman, use a clear container ("catch cup method") rather than bare hands.
  • Watch for signs of stress like raised front legs or sudden sprints, those mean “back off.”

Spotting Stress and Health Issues by Noticing Routine Changes

Healthy huntsmans have predictable routines: exploring at dusk, climbing regularly, accepting food soon after it’s offered. Sudden changes are signals something’s wrong.

Check these cues:

  • Eats within 24 hours of offering prey (when not molting)
  • Active climbing most evenings
  • Molts cleanly (no stuck limbs)
  • Abdomen looks full but not swollen

If you see frantic pacing, extended hiding during normal activity hours, or loss of appetite after changing something (like switching feeder insects), revert that change and wait to see if things improve before trying anything else.

Responding Quickly When Things Go Wrong

Mistakes happen, I’ve dealt with flooded substrate from over-misting, brief escapes thanks to tiny gaps, and missed feedings during busy weeks. The key is responding based on what you see right now instead of following rigid instructions.

For escapes: Turn off lights at night and search ceiling corners with a flashlight; huntsmans instinctively climb upward when loose.
For humidity spikes: Open vents immediately rather than waiting, your spider may move higher in the enclosure if conditions below are too damp.

Simple Steps for New Owners Tonight

Instead of guessing what your huntsman wants, spend ten quiet minutes watching it at dusk tonight. Note where it climbs first, how long it rests in certain spots, if it ignores certain hides or decorations, and use those notes as your guide for any changes tomorrow.

When you focus on actual behavior rather than checklists, caring for a huntsman becomes much simpler, and more rewarding. Every adjustment gets easier when you trust what you see right in front of you.
