Most identification guides feel written for calm, daylight encounters in the garden, not for startled people at midnight. So here’s what actually helps when you’re face-to-face with a mystery spider and need answers fast.

What Really Sets a Huntsman Apart (and Where Guides Go Wrong)

“Big and Flat”, But How Big?

Huntsmans are often described as “large and flat,” with legs splayed out like spokes. In reality, size can be deceptive under stress. That spider that looked as wide as a dinner plate? Probably closer to 4-5 inches across (legs included), with the body itself rarely more than 1-2 inches long.

Quick tip: If you can’t measure safely, compare the spider to something nearby, a light switch, your phone, or even your hand (from a distance). This grounds your estimate and keeps panic in check.

Leg Bands: Sometimes Obvious, Sometimes Not

Field guides love to mention leg striping or bands. In truth, some huntsmans have bold black bands; others are almost a solid dusty brown or gray. I once found one in my kitchen that looked like an old leaf, no stripes at all.

What matters more: Huntsmans always have legs that bend sharply forward and outward. If the bands are missing or faint, rely on the leg shape and how the spider holds itself against the wall.

Sideways Movement: The Real Give-Away

Here’s where things get clear. Huntsmans move in a very distinctive way, they scuttle sideways, hugging walls and slipping behind picture frames with ease. Wolf spiders and other common lookalikes tend to run straight ahead.

If you see that crab-like side-step: You’re almost certainly looking at a huntsman, even if color and pattern seem off.

The Challenges of Real-Life ID: Lighting, Nerves, and Juveniles

Poor Lighting Makes Everything Harder

Under dim lights or shadows, spiders look bigger, darker, and their markings blur together. Advice about “eye arrangement” is useless unless you’re extremely close, a risk not worth taking for most people.

Workaround: Use your phone to snap photos from several angles (even if blurry). Later, you can zoom in on details like leg posture or body shape without getting too close.

Small Huntsmans Fool Everyone

Juvenile huntsmans are much smaller, sometimes less than 2 inches across, and lighter in color than adults. Some species stay pale beige their whole lives. They flatten themselves against surfaces just like adults but can easily be mistaken for other harmless house spiders.

If it’s small but moves sideways: Still likely a huntsman. Focus on movement and flatness over color or size alone.

Quick Checklist: Is This Really a Huntsman Spider?

Here’s what I run through when someone sends me an “urgent spider photo”:

  • How did it move?
    Sideways = huntsman; straight dash = usually wolf spider.
  • Where was it hiding?
    Wedged behind frames or flat against walls points to huntsman.
  • Leg bands visible?
    Strong evidence if present, but don’t rule out huntsman if missing.
  • Body/leg span compared to everyday objects?
    If fully grown spans more than 3 inches with legs outstretched, not likely a recluse or common house spider.
  • Leg posture:
    Legs bent forward/outward (“crab pose”) is classic huntsman.

Keep this list handy, jot it down or save it on your phone for next time.

When Standard Advice Fails: A Composite Example

A reader once thought they’d found a brown recluse because their visitor was brown and fast. But brown recluses are small (body less than half an inch), don’t have banded legs, and never do the sideways crawl. The key factor missed? Movement style and body shape, not just color.

Lesson learned: Don’t trust color alone; focus on how the spider moves and holds its legs.

What To Do Next (Even If You’re Scared)

  1. Don’t panic, step back safely: Take a deep breath before approaching for photos.
  2. Grab your phone: Snap several pictures from different angles, details matter more than perfect focus.
  3. Compare to trusted sources: Good options include Australian Museum (link), Queensland Museum (link), or local university sites.
  4. Check movement style: A short video clip can confirm that sideways scuttle if you’re unsure.
  5. Ask local experts if needed: Many regions have Facebook groups or university extension offices happy to help ID spiders from photos.
  6. Relocate only if necessary, never touch directly: Use a clear glass/jar plus stiff card; keep fingers away from gaps since these spiders are fast and surprisingly flat!

Encouragement for Next Time

It’s normal to feel nervous spotting something so big indoors, but most huntsmans are harmless pest controllers who want nothing to do with humans. With practice, you’ll recognize their silhouette and sideways walk right away.

Remember:

  • Movement trumps color every time
  • Trust objects for scale over memory
  • Photos beat panicked guesses
  • Curiosity grows faster than fear

Next time you catch sight of those splayed legs hugging your wall, try observation before worry, you might start seeing them less as invaders and more as fascinating neighbors who keep other pests in check.

And if all else fails: take a breath, take a photo, ask someone who knows spiders well, and know you handled it better than most people ever will.
